# Analog Noise Analysis and Energy Resolution

## 1. Purpose

Basis for analyzing analog front-end (AFE) noise.

---

## 2. Definition of Noise Metrics

### 2.1 Output RMS Noise

Output RMS noise is defined as:

$$
V_{rms} = \sqrt{\int_{f_{low}}^{f_{high}} S_v^2(f) \, df}
$$

Where:

- $S_v(f)$ is voltage noise spectral density in V/sqrt(Hz)
- $f_{low}$ and $f_{high}$ define the effective bandwidth

The integration limits must be explicitly defined because RMS noise
is bandwidth dependent.

---

### 2.2 Differential Noise

For a differential ADC input:

$$
V_{diff}(t) = V_P(t) - V_N(t)
$$

All RMS noise calculations relevant to ADC performance
shall be performed on the differential signal:

$$
V_{diff,rms}
$$

---

## 2.3 Bandwidth Definition

Noise integration bandwidth must match the system sampling domain.

For an ADC operating at sampling frequency $f_s$:

$$
f_{Nyquist} = \frac{f_s}{2}
$$

Noise shall be integrated from DC to Nyquist frequency,
unless an analog anti-alias filter limits the effective bandwidth.

If an anti-alias filter is present, its transfer function
must be included in the noise model:

$$
S_{v,shaped}(f) = |H(f)|^2 \cdot S_v(f)
$$

---

## 2.4 Noise Spectral Density Integration

Noise spectral density ($S_v$) is expressed in units of:

$$\text{V} / \sqrt{\text{Hz}}$$

To compute the total **RMS noise** ($V_{rms}$) over a defined frequency bandwidth from $f_{low}$ to $f_{high}$:

$$V_{rms} = \sqrt{\int_{f_{low}}^{f_{high}} S_v^2(f) \, df}$$

### Key principles:
* The spectral density must be squared before integration.
* Integration must be performed in the linear domain.
* Units consistency must be preserved.

---

### 2.5 Input-Referred Noise (Charge Amplifier)

For a charge-sensitive amplifier (CSA):

$$
V_{out} = \frac{Q_{in}}{C_f}
$$

Where:

- $Q_{in}$ is the input charge
- $C_f$ is the feedback capacitor

Thus, the input-referred charge noise is:

$$
Q_{noise,rms} = V_{noise,rms} \cdot C_f
$$

Convert to equivalent number of electrons:

$$
N_e = \frac{Q_{noise,rms}}{e}
$$

Where:

- $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ C is the elementary charge

### Key Principles

1. Input-referred noise for a CSA is expressed in coulombs or electrons.
2. Smaller $C_f$ reduces output noise but decreases dynamic range.
3. Equivalent Noise Charge (ENC) is the standard metric used to quantify the noise performance of a charge-sensitive amplifier.
4. ENC allows direct comparison between detector resolution and electronic noise.
5. Input-referred charge noise is independent of detector capacitance.


## 2.6 Input-Referred Noise (TIA)
For a transimpedance amplifier (TIA):
$$V_{out} = I_{in} \cdot R_f$$

Thus, the input-referred current noise is:
$$I_{noise,rms} = \frac{V_{noise,rms}}{R_f}$$

**Where:**
* $R_f$: Feedback resistor.
* $V_{noise,rms}$: Output RMS noise.

Input-referred noise is the relevant metric for detector comparison.

---

## 2.7 RSS Combination of Independent Noise Sources
If multiple independent noise sources exist (AFE noise, ADC intrinsic noise, Reference noise, Detector shot noise), the total RMS noise is:

$$V_{total,rms} = \sqrt{V_1^2 + V_2^2 + V_3^2 + \dots}$$

**This assumes:**
* Statistical independence.
* Gaussian distribution.
* No deterministic correlation.
* **Note:** Noise powers add, not amplitudes.

---

## 2.8 Cumulative Integrated Noise
To evaluate the noise contribution versus frequency:

$$V_{rms}(f) = \sqrt{\int_{f_{low}}^{f} S_v^2(f') \, df'}$$

**This representation allows:**
* Identification of dominant frequency regions.
* Validation of bandwidth assumptions.
* Verification of anti-alias filtering impact.

---

## 2.9 Conversion to ENOB
Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR):

$$SNR = 20 \log_{10} \left( \frac{V_{signal,rms}}{V_{noise,rms}} \right)$$

Effective Number of Bits (ENOB):

$$ENOB = \frac{SNR - 1.76}{6.02}$$

**Where:**
* $1.76$ dB arises from ideal quantization error.
* $6.02$ dB per bit arises from $20 \log_{10}(2)$.

System ENOB is limited by the dominant noise source.

---

## 2.10 Equivalent Noise Charge (ENC)
Input-referred current noise can be converted to charge noise:

$$Q_{noise} = I_{noise,rms} \cdot \tau$$

**Where:**
* $\tau$: Shaping time constant.

Convert to electrons ($N_e$):

$$N_e = \frac{Q_{noise}}{e}$$

**Where:**
* $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ C.

ENC provides a direct linkage between electronic noise and spectroscopy energy resolution.

---

## 2.11 Impact on Energy Resolution
Energy resolution (FWHM) degradation due to noise:

$$\Delta E_{noise} \propto ENC$$

Total resolution combines statistical detector variance and electronic noise variance in quadrature:

$$\sigma_{total}^2 = \sigma_{detector}^2 + \sigma_{electronics}^2$$

# Clock Jitter and SNR Limitation

## 1. Purpose

Theoretical basis for clock jitter analysis.

---

## 2. Definitions

### 2.1 Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)

In power terms:

$$
\mathrm{SNR} = \frac{P_{\mathrm{signal}}}{P_{\mathrm{noise}}}
$$

In decibel form:

$$
\mathrm{SNR}_{dB} = 10 \log_{10}\left(\frac{P_{\mathrm{signal}}}{P_{\mathrm{noise}}}\right)
$$

For voltage quantities (equal impedance):

$$
\mathrm{SNR}_{dB} = 20 \log_{10}\left(\frac{V_{\mathrm{signal,rms}}}{V_{\mathrm{noise,rms}}}\right)
$$

---

### 2.2 RMS Clock Jitter

RMS jitter represents the statistical standard deviation of sampling time uncertainty.

If an ideal sampling instant occurs at time $t_0$, the actual sampling time is:

$$
t = t_0 + \Delta t
$$

where $\Delta t$ is a random timing deviation.

The RMS jitter is defined as:

$$
\sigma_t = \sqrt{E[(\Delta t)^2]}
$$

where $E[\cdot]$ denotes the expectation operator.

---

## 2.3 Derivation of Jitter-Limited SNR

Consider a sinusoidal input signal:

$$
x(t) = A \sin(2\pi f_{in} t)
$$

If sampling occurs with time error $\Delta t$, the amplitude error is:

$$
\Delta x = \frac{dx}{dt} \cdot \Delta t
$$

The derivative of the signal is:

$$
\frac{dx}{dt} = A \cdot 2\pi f_{in} \cos(2\pi f_{in} t)
$$

Thus:

$$
\Delta x = A \cdot 2\pi f_{in} \cos(2\pi f_{in} t) \cdot \Delta t
$$

The RMS value of $\cos(\cdot)$ is $1/\sqrt{2}$.

Therefore, jitter-induced RMS noise is:

$$
V_{\mathrm{jitter,rms}} = \frac{A}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot 2\pi f_{in} \sigma_t
$$

Signal RMS:

$$
V_{\mathrm{signal,rms}} = \frac{A}{\sqrt{2}}
$$

Thus:

$$
\mathrm{SNR} = \frac{V_{\mathrm{signal,rms}}}{V_{\mathrm{jitter,rms}}}
= \frac{1}{2\pi f_{in} \sigma_t}
$$

In decibel form:

$$
\mathrm{SNR}_{dB} = -20 \log_{10}(2\pi f_{in} \sigma_t)
$$

This shows jitter-induced SNR degradation increases linearly with input frequency.

---

## 2.4 Derivation of ENOB Formula

For an ideal N-bit ADC with full-scale peak-to-peak range $V_{FS,pp}$:

$$
\mathrm{LSB} = \frac{V_{FS,pp}}{2^N}
$$

Quantization noise RMS (uniform distribution):

$$
V_{q,rms} = \frac{\mathrm{LSB}}{\sqrt{12}}
$$

For a full-scale sine wave:

$$
V_{\mathrm{signal,rms}} = \frac{V_{FS,pp}}{2\sqrt{2}}
$$

Thus:

```math
\mathrm{SNR} =
\frac{V_{\mathrm{signal,rms}}}{V_{q,rms}}
=
\frac{\frac{V_{FS,pp}}{2\sqrt{2}}}
{\frac{V_{FS,pp}}{\sqrt{12} \cdot 2^N}}
=
\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}} \cdot 2^N
```

In decibel form, the ideal SNR for an $N$-bit converter is:

$$SNR_{dB} = 6.02N + 1.76$$

Rearranging to solve for the **Effective Number of Bits (ENOB)** based on measured SNR:

$$ENOB = \frac{SNR_{dB} - 1.76}{6.02}$$


## 2.5 Total SNR Combination
When multiple independent noise sources exist (e.g., ADC intrinsic noise and jitter noise), noise powers add in the linear domain.

If:

$$SNR_{ADC}$$
$$SNR_{jitter}$$

Then:

$$\frac{1}{SNR_{total,linear}} = \frac{1}{SNR_{ADC,linear}} + \frac{1}{SNR_{jitter,linear}}$$

Where:
$$SNR_{linear} = 10^{SNR_{dB}/10}$$

Convert back:

$$SNR_{total,dB} = 10 \log_{10}(SNR_{total,linear})$$

Noise powers must always be summed in linear scale.

---

## 2.6 RSS Jitter Combination
If multiple independent RMS jitter sources exist:

$$\sigma_{total} = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 + \sigma_3^2 + \dots}$$

This follows from variance addition:

$$\sigma_{total}^2 = \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 + \sigma_3^2 + \dots$$

Valid only for uncorrelated jitter sources.

# ENOB and ADC Performance Limitations

## 1. Purpose
This document defines the relationship between nominal ADC resolution ($N$ bits), intrinsic ADC SNR, and achievable system ENOB. It establishes why system requirements must be derived from real ADC performance rather than ideal quantization assumptions.

---

## 2. Definitions

### 2.1 Ideal Quantization SNR
For an ideal $N$-bit ADC:

$$SNR_{ideal} = 6.02N + 1.76 \text{ dB}$$

This represents the theoretical limit assuming quantization noise is the only error source.

---

### 2.2 Correct Engineering Approach
The system ENOB must satisfy:

$$ENOB_{system} \le ENOB_{ADC}$$

A practical requirement must include a margin ($\Delta$):

$$ENOB_{system} \ge ENOB_{ADC} - \Delta$$

Where $\Delta$ is typically $0.2$ to $0.5$ bits to account for:
* Analog front-end noise
* Bandwidth limitations
* Clock jitter
* Anti-alias filtering
* Implementation/thermal variation

---

### 2.3 Deriving Noise Requirement from ENOB
Given a target ENOB, we find the required SNR:

$$SNR = 6.02 \cdot ENOB + 1.76$$

Using the relationship to RMS voltage:

$$SNR = 20 \log_{10} \left( \frac{V_{signal,rms}}{V_{noise,rms}} \right)$$

Solving for the allowable noise floor ($V_{noise,max}$):

$$V_{noise,max} = \frac{V_{signal,rms}}{10^{SNR/20}}$$

# SiPM and Charge Amplifier Readout

## 1. Purpose
Theoretical background of the Silicon Photomultiplier (SiPM) and its readout using a charge‑sensitive amplifier.

---
## 2. Definitions

### 2.1 Silicon Photomultiplier (SiPM) Operation

When a photon is absorbed in a microcell, it may trigger an avalanche process, producing a fixed amount of charge. The charge generated by a single avalanche event is given by:

$$Q_{1ph} = G \cdot q_e$$

**Where:**
* $G$: SiPM gain (number of charge carriers generated during one avalanche event).
* $q_e$: Elementary charge ($1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ C).

> **Note:** The gain $G$ is typically provided in the device datasheet and depends on the applied overvoltage.

For multiple detected photons:

$$Q_{total} = N \cdot G \cdot q_e$$

**Where:**
* $N$: Number of triggered microcells (approximately equal to detected photons under low light conditions).

Thus, the SiPM behaves as a quantized charge generator.

---

### 2.2 SiPM Capacitance
Since all microcells are connected in parallel, the SiPM exhibits a total equivalent capacitance ($C_{SiPM}$). This capacitance arises from the junction capacitance of each microcell and the parallel combination of all cells.

$C_{SiPM}$ influences:
* Bandwidth
* Noise
* Stability of the front-end amplifier

In a charge amplifier configuration, however, the gain does not depend directly on $C_{SiPM}$.

---

### 2.3 Microcell Recharge Time Constant
After an avalanche event, each microcell must recharge through its internal quench resistor. This follows exponential recovery behavior characterized by the microcell time constant:

$$\tau_{cell} = R_q \cdot C_{cell}$$

**Where:**
* $R_q$: Internal quench resistor.
* $C_{cell}$: Microcell capacitance.

> **Note:** The gain $\tau_{cell}$ is typically provided in the device datasheet.

The value of $\tau_{cell}$ affects the maximum count rate, pulse shape, and dynamic response.

---

### 2.4 Charge Amplifier Principle
To convert the SiPM charge pulse into a measurable voltage, a charge-sensitive amplifier (CSA) is used. In an ideal charge amplifier:

$$V_{out} = \frac{Q_{in}}{C_f}$$

Substituting the SiPM charge expression:

$$V_{out} = \frac{N \cdot G \cdot q_e}{C_f}$$

**This shows that:**
1. The output voltage is proportional to the number of detected photons.
2. The gain is determined only by the feedback capacitor $C_f$.
3. The detector capacitance $C_{SiPM}$ does not set the signal amplitude.

---

### 2.5 Feedback Capacitor ($C_f$)
In a charge-sensitive amplifier (CSA), the output voltage is determined by the input charge and the feedback capacitance:

$$V_{out} = \frac{Q_{in}}{C_f}$$

For an SiPM, the input charge generated by $N$ detected photons is:

$$Q_{in} = N \cdot G \cdot q_e$$

Combining both equations:

$$V_{out} = \frac{N \cdot G \cdot q_e}{C_f}$$

Rearranging to determine the required feedback capacitance for a specific target voltage:

$$C_f = \frac{N \cdot G \cdot q_e}{V_{out}}$$

**Thus, the feedback capacitor is selected based on:**
* Expected maximum number of detected photons ($N$).
* SiPM gain ($G$) from the datasheet.
* Desired output voltage amplitude ($V_{out}$).

The value of $C_f$ directly sets the charge-to-voltage conversion gain of the system.

---

### 2.6 Feedback Resistor ($R_f$)
The feedback resistor determines the discharge rate of the feedback capacitor and therefore the pulse decay time. The decay time constant of the charge amplifier is:

$$\tau = R_f \cdot C_f$$

To ensure accurate charge integration, the amplifier time constant is chosen relative to the SiPM microcell recharge time constant ($\tau_{cell}$):

$$\tau \ge k \cdot \tau_{cell}$$

**Where:**
* $\tau_{cell}$ is provided in the datasheet.
* $k$ is typically selected between 3 and 5.

Solving for the feedback resistor:

$$R_f = \frac{\tau}{C_f}$$

Therefore, once $C_f$ is determined and the desired shaping time $\tau$ is selected, the required feedback resistor can be calculated.

---

### 2.7 Design Considerations
* **$C_f$**: Defines signal amplitude and dynamic range. Larger capacitance prevents saturation but lowers sensitivity.
* **$R_f$**: Defines pulse width and maximum count rate capability. Lower resistance allows for faster counting but may increase electronic noise.
* Both parameters must be selected considering noise, bandwidth, and stability constraints of the operational amplifier.












### 2.X Detector Shot Noise (SiPM)
The dominant intrinsic noise mechanism of a SiPM is shot noise associated with dark current. The RMS shot noise current is given by:
$$i_{shot,rms} = \sqrt{2q I_{dark} \Delta f}$$

**Where:**
* $q$: Elementary charge ($1.602 \times 10^{-19} \text{ C}$).
* $I_{dark}$: Detector dark current.
* $\Delta f$: Effective noise bandwidth.

Shot noise arises from the discrete nature of charge carriers and follows Poisson statistics.

**Key Properties:**
* Shot noise is proportional to the square root of dark current.
* Shot noise increases with bandwidth.
* Shot noise is statistically independent from electronic amplifier noise.

This contribution must be included in the total noise RSS calculation when modeling full system performance.

---

### Effective Noise Bandwidth
The effective noise integration bandwidth is not defined by arbitrary frequency limits, but by the complete system transfer function:
$$H_{system}(f) = H_{AFE}(f) \cdot H_{filter}(f)$$

The integrated noise is therefore determined by:
* **Analog front-end transfer function:** The gain and bandwidth of the CSA/TIA.
* **Anti-alias filtering:** The roll-off characteristics of the hardware filters.
* **ADC Nyquist limit:** The sampling rate constraints.

Noise integration must reflect the true signal chain frequency response.

---

### Generalized Noise Integration
In the most rigorous form, RMS noise is computed as:
$$V_{n,rms} = \sqrt{\int_{0}^{\infty} |H(f)|^2 \cdot e_n^2(f) \, df}$$

**Where:**
* $e_n(f)$: The noise spectral density.
* $H(f)$: Represents the complete system transfer function.
